## 📝 Transcript Content & Summary

To stop AI slop, the video suggests moving away from banning individual words (a "whack-a-mole" approach) and instead implementing a systemic writing standard that the model can check itself against (1:15, 12:44).

Key takeaways on fixing AI writing:

Use a Controlled Language System: The narrator highlights ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English), a standard originally built in 1986 for aircraft mechanics to remove ambiguity (1:40, 6:56). It uses a restricted dictionary and rigid grammar rules to eliminate common AI habits like synonym rotation, excessive hedging, and run-on sentences (5:53-6:53).
Why it works: Slop is essentially "ambiguity with good posture." A system like STE acts as a mechanical constraint that forces the model to prioritize clarity over flowery or hollow phrasing (7:04, 12:46).
Practical Application: You don't need to paste the entire manual. The video suggests distilling the rules into an agent skill or linter that enforces sentence-length caps, dictionary limits, and active voice requirements (13:44).
Caveat: STE fixes the form of the writing (removing slop), but not the substance (the truth of the content). Use it where clarity is the primary job—like technical documentation, error messages, or pull requests—but keep it away from tasks that require a unique creative voice (14:51, 15:32).
